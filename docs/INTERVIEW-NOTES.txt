================================================================================
PART 1 - THE 60-SECOND ANSWER
================================================================================

"Every self-driving car software stack in the world plans inside a lane. It
takes the lane centre line as its reference, and it treats the world as a grid
of occupied and free cells. On an Indian road neither of those exists. There
are no reliable lane markings, and the traffic is mixed - buses, autos,
two-wheelers filtering through 40 cm gaps, pushcarts, pedestrians, cattle.

So I removed both assumptions. Instead of a lane centre line, the planner
solves for a 'drivable corridor' - a dynamic program over the free space ahead,
recomputed twenty times a second. Instead of an occupancy grid, it builds a
continuous risk field that knows what each road user *is*, how badly hitting it
would hurt, and where it might be two seconds from now.

Around that I built the whole loop: simulated camera, LiDAR and radar with
noise, dropout and occlusion; Kalman tracking; multi-modal prediction; an
eight-state behaviour machine; a jerk-minimal trajectory lattice; and an RSS
safety supervisor that can only ever slow the vehicle down.

It runs at 20 Hz on a laptop, and I measured it: ten scenarios, three seeds,
against a conventional lane-following planner on identical seeds. It gets 43%
of the way down the road where the baseline gets 36%, and it wins on eight of
the ten scenarios. It is not yet safer by raw contact count, and I can tell you
exactly why."

That last sentence is the most important one in the whole answer. It signals
you know your own numbers instead of selling them.


================================================================================
PART 2 - THE PROBLEM, AND WHY IT IS ACTUALLY HARD
================================================================================

[KID]
Imagine you're walking through a really crowded market. Nobody walks in neat
lines. There are cows, bikes squeezing past you, people crossing wherever they
want, and holes in the road. Now imagine you had to write down *rules* for how
to walk through it - so precise that a robot could follow them, and never bump
into anyone. That's the problem.

[REAL]
The problem statement (SIH26037) names six things, and each is a scoring line:

  1. Adaptive path planning     - the path changes as the scene changes
  2. Collision avoidance        - with dynamic, unpredictable obstacles
  3. Unstructured roads         - missing/unreliable markings, mixed traffic
  4. Replanning latency         - they want a number, in milliseconds
  5. Path smoothness            - jerk and curvature, not just "it didn't crash"
  6. MathWorks toolchain        - MATLAB, Simulink, Stateflow, RoadRunner

Why it is genuinely hard, and not just "add more sensors":

  * Lane markings are not merely missing - they are sometimes WRONG. A planner
    that trusts a faded marking is worse than one that ignores markings.
  * Traffic is non-lane-based. Two vehicles can be side by side with 30 cm
    between them and both consider that normal. Standard car-following models
    (which assume a queue in a lane) produce nonsense here.
  * The classes matter. Hitting a barricade and hitting a child are not the
    same event, but a binary occupancy grid says they are.
  * Behaviour is multi-modal. A two-wheeler beside you is either holding
    station or about to filter into the gap in front of you. Those futures are
    metres apart. Predicting the average of them is confidently wrong.
  * There is no clean "right of way". At an unsignalled junction everyone
    negotiates. A planner that waits for a guaranteed gap waits forever.

INTERVIEW TIP: if you are asked "why not just use an existing stack" - the
answer is that existing stacks are not badly tuned for India, they are
structurally incompatible with it. Their core data structure (the lane) does
not exist here.

PART 3 - THE ONE BIG IDEA
================================================================================

If you remember one thing, remember this table:

  A conventional stack uses      | SARATHI uses instead
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Lane centre line as the        | Drivable corridor - a dynamic program over
  planning reference             | free space, re-solved every tick
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Binary occupancy grid          | Risk field - continuous, class-conditioned,
                                 | harm-weighted, indexed by time
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  One predicted path per object  | Multi-modal intent, covariance grows with
                                 | time
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Obstacles are walls            | A pothole is traversable cost; the verge is
                                 | drivable
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Lane-change state machine      | Eight behaviours, wrong-way evasion included
  -------------------------------|--------------------------------------------
  Fixed safety envelope          | RSS recalibrated to Indian gap acceptance

THE SENTENCE:
  "We did not add Indian conditions to a Western planner. We removed the
   assumption the Western planner is built on."

WHY THIS IS THE INTERESTING PART:
A MathWorks judge knows the Frenet trajectory-planning example in the Automated
Driving Toolbox by heart. If your answer sounds like that example, you are one
of forty teams. That example has a lattice; it does not have a corridor solver
or a risk field. Start your technical answer at the corridor and the risk
field, not at the lattice.


================================================================================
PART 4 - THE PIPELINE, STAGE BY STAGE
================================================================================

Eight stages, executed in this order, twenty times a second (every 50 ms):

  SENSE -> FUSE -> PREDICT -> RISK FIELD -> CORRIDOR -> BEHAVIOUR -> LATTICE
        -> RSS + CBF

Most interview questions are secretly "which stage handles that?". Learn the
order and you can place any question.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 1 - SENSE            sarathi/perception/sensors.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
The car has three kinds of eyes, and all three are a bit broken in different
ways. One can tell a cow from a motorbike but is bad at judging distance. One
measures distance perfectly but has no idea what it's looking at. One is great
at speed but can't tell left from right very well. And if a bus is in the way,
none of them can see behind it.

[REAL]
Three sensor models, each with the failure mode that actually matters for that
modality:

  Camera - classifies objects (the only sensor that can), but misses small and
           distant ones, and its position error grows with range.
  LiDAR  - accurate geometry, NO class label, hard occlusion shadow.
  Radar  - long range, direct range-rate (speed) measurement, poor lateral
           resolution: it knows how far, not exactly where across.

Noise is ANISOTROPIC - depth error and lateral error are different sizes. This
matters: modelling both as one circle is the shortcut that makes a demo look
better than the system is.

Occlusion is geometric: each obstacle claims an angular interval as seen from
the sensor, and anything behind it in that interval is simply not detected.

[WHY IT MATTERS - say this in an interview]
"The planner has no access to the simulator's ground truth. It sees tracks,
with the errors a real detector makes. That is why the risk field has an
uncertainty skirt at all - if we planned on ground truth, that skirt would be
decoration."

Planning on ground truth is the single most common way a student demo lies
about its own capability.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 2 - FUSE             sarathi/perception/fusion.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
Three sets of broken eyes see the same cow and each reports something slightly
different. This step decides "those three blobs are all one cow", remembers the
cow between frames, and keeps a running opinion about whether it's a cow or a
motorbike.

[REAL]
  * Constant-velocity KALMAN FILTER per track. State = position and velocity.
    Predict forward, then correct with new detections.
  * Data association by a GATED nearest neighbour: a detection can only update
    a track if it lands within 3 sigma of the predicted position (gate clamped
    between 1 m and 8 m).
  * Track LIFECYCLE: a new detection creates a "tentative" track; 2 hits
    confirm it; 12 consecutive misses delete it. In between it COASTS - keeps
    predicting without measurements. Coasting is what lets the car keep braking
    for a cow that just disappeared behind a bus.
  * CLASS FUSION IN LOG-ODDS (see PART 5). Not averaging probabilities.

[CODE - the log-odds bit, explained simply]
Instead of storing "70% sure it's a cow", store a score. Every time the camera
says "cow, 80% confident", add log(0.8/0.2) = +1.39 to the cow score and
subtract a little from the others. To get probabilities back out, softmax the
scores.

Two reasons this is better than averaging:
  1. Evidence ADDS. Ten weak sightings can add up to a strong belief.
  2. Two confident, disagreeing observations do not average to "no idea".

Scores are BOUNDED to +/-40 so that a belief built over a long run can still be
corrected by contrary evidence. An unbounded belief at +500 is unfalsifiable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 3 - PREDICT          sarathi/prediction/intent.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
For every person, bike or cow, guess what they'll do next. But don't guess just
ONE thing - guess all the things they might do, and how likely each is. A cow
might stand still, or wander left, or suddenly bolt. All three get drawn.

[REAL]
Six manoeuvre hypotheses per tracked object:

  CONTINUE   - hold speed and lateral position
  CUT_IN     - move laterally into our path
  FILTER     - thread through a gap (two-wheelers, autos)
  STOP       - decelerate to standstill
  DART       - sudden large lateral movement (pedestrians, animals)
  WRONG_WAY  - travelling against the corridor direction

The prior probability of each is CLASS-CONDITIONED. A two-wheeler filters; a
bus essentially never does. A pedestrian darts; a barricade never moves.

Each hypothesis is propagated forward in the corridor's Frenet frame (so
predicted paths follow the road, they don't fly off a curve), and carries a
covariance that GROWS with time - because you know less about 3 seconds from
now than about 0.5 seconds from now - and is CLAMPED to what the object can
physically reach. A stationary car cannot be 1.8 m sideways in one second.

[WHY MULTI-MODAL - the killer line]
"Committing to the mean of two futures that are metres apart produces a
prediction that is confidently wrong, which is worse than no prediction."

[HONEST CAVEAT - say it before they ask]
The priors are hand-built from observed Indian driving, not learned from a
trajectory dataset. With data we would fit them per class from IDD (the India
Driving Dataset from IIIT Hyderabad).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 4 - THE RISK FIELD   sarathi/planning/risk.py   <- THE CENTREPIECE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
Instead of drawing a box around each thing and saying "don't go in the box",
we paint the whole road with heat. Bright red where it's very dangerous, fading
to nothing where it's safe. A cow paints a big fuzzy red cloud because we don't
know what it'll do. A parked car paints a small sharp one. A child paints a
brighter one than a lamp post, because hurting a child is worse. And we paint a
separate picture for "now", "half a second from now", "one second from now",
and so on.

[REAL]
Four ingredients:

  1. PREDICTED OCCUPANCY, summed over every manoeuvre hypothesis weighted by
     its probability. So the field IS the expected cost, not the cost of the
     single most likely future.
  2. CLASS-CONDITIONED ANISOTROPIC KERNELS. Not circles - ellipse-ish shapes
     longer along the direction of travel than across it, sized by the object's
     footprint plus its predicted uncertainty.
  3. HARM WEIGHTING. Explicit table in the code:
        pedestrian 1.00, bicycle 0.95, two-wheeler 0.92, cattle 0.80,
        pushcart 0.78, auto 0.70, bus/truck 0.66, car 0.60,
        parked vehicle 0.50, barricade 0.40
     This is a deliberate ethical choice and you should say so out loud: an
     unprotected road user costs the planner more than sheet metal.
  4. TRAVERSABLE TERRAIN COST. Potholes raise cost without ever becoming
     impassable, so the car crosses a pothole slowly instead of swerving into
     oncoming traffic to avoid it.

[THE KERNEL SHAPE - a genuinely good detail to know]
Each kernel has a FLAT TOP and a GAUSSIAN SKIRT.

  CORE  = the configuration-space footprint: the object's body + the ego's body
          + a clearance margin. Inside it, risk is saturated (uniformly
          maximal).
  SKIRT = the prediction uncertainty. It controls how fast risk DECAYS outside
          the core - not how big the core is.

Why flat-topped? If the kernel peaked at the centre, there would be a spurious
gradient pulling the planner *towards* the obstacle's centre from inside it.
Flat means no gradient inside the core.

Why keep core and skirt separate? Folding sigma into the core (the tempting
shortcut) inflates a bus into a 3.6 m half-width wall four seconds out and
closes roads that are in fact passable. We tried it. It closed roads.

[TWO SEPARATE OUTPUTS - important]
  agent_risk()  - a soft cost used to RANK trajectories.
  penetration() - a hard geometric depth of intrusion used to FORBID them.

The soft field must not be asked to forbid, because harm weighting means a
near-miss with a pedestrian legitimately scores worse than a collision with a
barricade - which is right for ranking and wrong for feasibility.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 5 - THE CORRIDOR     sarathi/planning/corridor_path.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
Draw an invisible line down the road showing where it's actually possible to
drive - going around the parked truck, the barricade, the cow that's standing
still. Not the painted line. The line that a good driver would take. Redraw it
every time anything moves.

[REAL]
A DYNAMIC PROGRAM over a 2-D grid:
  state  = (distance along the road s, lateral offset d)
  grid   = 70 m ahead, 2.5 m longitudinal steps, 0.3 m lateral steps
  cost   = blockage overlap (60) + corridor-edge proximity (25)
           + keep-left preference (0.35) + lateral smoothness (5.0)
  moves  = any lateral change per step whose rate is under the vehicle's
           tracking limit; anything sharper is set to infinity

Standard DP: forward pass accumulating minimum cost, backward pass recovering
the chosen path. Vectorised in numpy - the whole transition matrix is one
broadcast subtraction.

Output is not just a line. It returns:
  * the reference path (a smooth curve the lattice can plan in)
  * a CLEARANCE profile (how much room there is)
  * which SIDE is free
The behaviour layer needs all three.

[THE HARD CLAMP - a bug fix that became a design rule]
The edge penalty is finite, so a heavy enough blockage could "buy its way" off
the carriageway. Now the profile is hard-clamped inside the corridor bounds.
Driving off the road is not a trade the planner may make.

[THIS IS THE CENTRAL CLAIM]
Because the reference is derived from free space, there is NO BRANCH ANYWHERE
IN THE CODE for "lane markings missing". Markings, when present, are only a
prior on the nominal offset - a weak preference, not a constraint.

[LIKELY QUESTION] "Isn't your corridor just a lane you computed?"
[ANSWER] "Yes - and that is the point. It is derived from free space every
tick instead of read from paint. When a bus stops in it, the corridor bends
around the bus. A lane cannot."

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 6 - BEHAVIOUR        sarathi/planning/behaviour.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
Before deciding exactly where to steer, decide what you're TRYING to do. Am I
cruising? Following someone? Squeezing past? Overtaking? Giving way? Crawling
in a crowd? Getting out of the way of someone coming at me head-on? Stopping
right now?

[REAL] Eight states, evaluated as guards in strict priority order:

  CRUISE           free road - hold the desired speed
  FOLLOW           matched to a leader's speed and gap
  NUDGE            squeezing past an obstruction, speed reduced
  OVERTAKE         leader is slow and there is width to pass
  YIELD            giving way at a junction or to a vulnerable road user
  CREEP            dense traffic - walking pace, maximum alertness
  WRONG_WAY_EVADE  a rider is coming head-on on our side
  EMERGENCY_STOP   time-to-collision below threshold - stop now

Every transition has a minimum DWELL TIME so the machine cannot chatter between
neighbouring states on sensor noise.

[WHY A SEPARATE LAYER AT ALL]
The lattice answers "which trajectory?". This layer answers "what am I trying
to do?". They must be separate because the same geometry means different
things: a stationary object 20 m ahead is a FOLLOW problem if it's a bus at a
stand, and an EVADE problem if it's a rider coming the wrong way.

[THE MATHWORKS ANGLE - use this]
The state set is deliberately small and explicitly enumerated because it maps
1:1 onto a Stateflow chart, and a chart is only readable if the states are.
That is the bridge to the MATLAB deliverable.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 7 - THE LATTICE      sarathi/planning/lattice.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
Draw a fan of a hundred possible paths the car could take in the next few
seconds. Throw away the ones that are physically impossible (too sharp, too
fast). Score every one that's left against the heat map, plus how comfortable
it is. Drive the best one. Do it all again 50 milliseconds later.

[REAL]
  * Sample TERMINAL STATES, not controls: lateral offsets across the corridor,
    terminal speeds, and manoeuvre durations (1.6, 2.6, 3.6 s).
  * Connect current state to each terminal state with a JERK-MINIMAL
    polynomial: quintic laterally (position boundary conditions), quartic
    longitudinally (velocity boundary conditions). See PART 5.
  * Reject candidates violating acceleration, braking, curvature or lateral
    acceleration limits.
  * Score survivors against the TIME-INDEXED risk field - each point of each
    trajectory is evaluated at the time the car would actually be there - plus
    jerk, curvature, offset from reference, and speed shortfall.
  * Everything is BATCHED in numpy: all candidates evaluated as one array
    operation, not in a Python loop.

[THE COST FUNCTION - weights are on screen and adjustable]
  w_risk 12.0, w_speed 2.4, w_offset 1.1, w_terminal_offset 2.0,
  w_jerk_lat 0.14, w_jerk_long 0.10

[SAFE-STOP FALLBACK]
There is always one guaranteed-feasible candidate: a comfortable
decelerate-to-stop. It is a LAST RESORT ONLY - it does not compete on cost.
(It used to, and it won, and the car stopped a lot. See PART 6.)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STAGE 8 - RSS + CONTROL BARRIER    sarathi/safety/rss.py
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID]
After the car has picked its plan, one last checker looks at it and says "no,
that's too fast for the gap you have" and slows it down. This checker can only
ever slow the car down, never speed it up. So even if everything else has a bug,
this layer cannot make the car more dangerous.

[REAL]
RSS (Responsibility-Sensitive Safety, from Mobileye) gives a formal minimum
safe distance given both speeds, a response time, and braking limits:

  d_safe = v_ego*rho + 0.5*a_max*rho^2       (distance covered while reacting)
         + (v_ego + rho*a_max)^2 / (2*b_min) (our braking distance)
         - v_front^2 / (2*b_front)           (credit for their braking)

We INVERT this in closed form to get a safe SPEED directly, instead of
proposing a speed and testing it. It is a quadratic in v_ego, so it's one
sqrt - cheap enough to run against every tracked object every tick.

There is a separate, harsher formula for OPPOSITE-direction traffic, where the
closing speed is the SUM of the two speeds. This is why a wrong-way rider is so
much more dangerous than a slow leader at the same range.

Then a CONTROL BARRIER FUNCTION filter caps the commanded acceleration as the
margin closes, with gain alpha = 1.5. Higher alpha is safer and jerkier.

[THE INDIA CALIBRATION - the interesting part]
  rho (response time)   0.6 s   (textbook highway RSS uses 0.75-1.0 s)
  a_max during response 1.8 m/s^2
  b_min (guaranteed)    3.6 m/s^2
  b_max (emergency)     6.5 m/s^2
  mu_lateral            0.25 m  (textbook uses 0.5 m or more)
  VRU lateral bonus     +0.35 m
  min_standoff          2.0 m

Applied unmodified to a Delhi market street, textbook RSS demands gaps that do
not exist, the filter saturates, and the vehicle never moves. That is not
safety, it is paralysis.

[min_standoff - a subtle one worth knowing]
RSS alone permits creeping to within centimetres of a stopped vehicle, because
at 0.09 m/s your stopping distance really is centimetres. Safe and useless: a
car wedged 0.6 m behind a stopped bus cannot build the forward travel a lateral
manoeuvre needs, so it can never pull out. Human drivers leave room precisely
so they can pull out. So do we.

[THE HARD QUESTION] "RSS assumes everyone else follows RSS. Nobody here does."
[ANSWER] "Correct. We use RSS as a lower bound on OUR OWN behaviour, not as a
prediction of theirs. Their behaviour is handled in the prediction stage, which
assumes they will do the worst plausible thing."

[THE PROPERTY THAT MATTERS]
Monotone by construction: it can only reduce speed. That single property is why
no tuning mistake upstream can make the vehicle more aggressive than this layer
permits. Say the word "monotone" - it is the whole argument in one word.

PART 5 - THE MATHS YOU MUST BE ABLE TO SKETCH ON A WHITEBOARD
================================================================================

You do not need to derive these. You need to be able to draw them and say what
they are for.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1 THE FRENET FRAME
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID] Instead of saying "I'm at latitude X, longitude Y", say "I'm 40 metres
along this road and 1.2 metres left of the middle". Much easier to think about.

[REAL] Coordinates relative to a reference curve:
  s = arc length along the curve (how far along the road)
  d = signed lateral offset (positive to the LEFT of travel direction)

Why: in (x, y) a road curve makes every equation messy. In (s, d), "stay in
your lane" is just "keep d small", and "go faster" is just "increase s-dot".
The road being curved disappears into the coordinate system.

Conversion needs finding the closest point on the polyline. Naively that is a
loop over every segment; we use a scipy cKDTree for nearest-neighbour lookup
plus a local refinement. That change alone was a ~34x speed-up on the batch
conversion, which mattered because we convert thousands of points per tick.

Cost of the trick: near a sharp curve, when |d| is large, the mapping can
become ill-conditioned. We keep d bounded by clamping to the corridor.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2 JERK-MINIMAL POLYNOMIALS (quintic and quartic)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID] If you want to move from here to there smoothly, without any sudden
yanks, there is exactly one "smoothest possible" way to do it, and maths can
write it down for you.

[REAL] Jerk is the rate of change of acceleration - it is what passengers feel
as a lurch. The trajectory that minimises integrated squared jerk between two
fully specified states is a QUINTIC polynomial (5th order) in time.

  LATERAL:      quintic. You specify start (d, d', d'') and end (d, d', d'').
                Six constraints -> six coefficients.
  LONGITUDINAL: quartic. You do NOT care where you end up along the road, only
                how fast you are going. So you specify start (s, s', s'') and
                end (s', s''). Five constraints -> five coefficients.

Both reduce to a small linear solve: A c = b, 3x3 for the quintic and 2x2 for
the quartic. That is literally what the code does (np.linalg.solve).

This is the Werling formulation (Werling et al., ICRA 2010) - cite it by name.

[THE DERIVATION YOU SHOULD KNOW - used to fix a real bug]
For the quartic with zero initial acceleration, velocity is a cubic and
ACCELERATION IS A PARABOLA peaking at the midpoint of the manoeuvre, with

  peak acceleration = 1.5 * delta_v / T

That means the reachable change in speed over duration T is bounded:

  delta_v_max = (2/3) * a_max * T

From a standstill with a_max = 2.0 m/s^2 and T = 1.6 s, the fastest terminal
speed you can actually reach is 2.13 m/s. Sampling 3.0 m/s is not ambition, it
is waste - the candidate is built, scored, and then thrown away. This closed
form is what fixed the biggest bug in the project (PART 6, bug 12).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.3 DYNAMIC PROGRAMMING (the corridor solver)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID] To find the best path through a grid, work out the best way to reach
every square in the first column, then use that to work out the best way to
reach every square in the second column, and so on. At the end, walk backwards
to see which route won.

[REAL] Classic shortest-path-on-a-DAG. Complexity O(n_s * n_d^2) - with 28
longitudinal steps (70 m at 2.5 m) and a few tens of lateral cells (corridor
width at 0.3 m), that is on the order of tens of thousands of transition
evaluations, done as one vectorised numpy operation per column.

Why DP and not A*? The state space here is a small regular grid with a natural
ordering (you always move forward along s), so there is no need for a priority
queue or a heuristic. DP is simpler, has no tie-breaking pathologies, and
vectorises trivially.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.4 THE KALMAN FILTER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID] You have a guess about where something is, and a new (slightly wrong)
measurement. The Kalman filter is the rule for combining them: trust the
measurement more when your guess is uncertain, trust your guess more when the
measurement is noisy.

[REAL] Two steps per frame:
  PREDICT: push the state forward with the motion model (here: constant
           velocity), and grow the uncertainty.
  UPDATE:  blend prediction and measurement, weighted by the Kalman gain, and
           shrink the uncertainty.

Constant-velocity is a deliberate choice: it is the right model when you have
no idea what the object intends. Intent is handled separately, in the
prediction stage, precisely so the filter can stay simple.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.5 LOG-ODDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  logit(p) = log(p / (1 - p))

Adding evidence in log space is multiplication in probability space, which is
what Bayes' rule does. So accumulating evidence becomes ADDITION - cheap, order
independent, and it never saturates at exactly 0 or 1.

Recovering probabilities uses SOFTMAX via the log-sum-exp trick: subtract the
maximum score before exponentiating, so exp() never overflows.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.6 IDM AND NLB-IDM (how the other traffic drives)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
[KID] A rule for how close a driver follows the car in front, and when they
brake. The normal version assumes everyone is in a queue in a lane. Indian
traffic isn't in lanes, so we changed the rule: how much you care about another
vehicle depends on how much you two OVERLAP sideways, not on which lane you're
in.

[REAL] IDM (Intelligent Driver Model) is the standard car-following model:

  accel = a * [ 1 - (v/v0)^delta - (s*(v, dv) / gap)^2 ]

  where s*(v, dv) = s0 + v*T + v*dv / (2*sqrt(a*b))

NLB-IDM (non-lane-based) changes ONE thing: the interaction is weighted by a
SEPARATION RATIO w in [0, 1] that is 1 when two footprints overlap laterally
and decays smoothly to 0 as the clear lateral gap grows past an "influence"
width (itself a function of speed - a fast rider claims a wider pseudo-lane).

On top of that:
  * class-specific lateral clearance (a two-wheeler passes a bus with 0.4 m; a
    car will not)
  * GAP SEEKING - riders actively hunt for gaps rather than queueing. This is
    what makes the market and bus-stop scenarios genuinely hard.

This is not decoration. If the surrounding traffic were polite, our planner
would look brilliant and mean nothing.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.7 CONTROL BARRIER FUNCTIONS (one paragraph version)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Define a function h(x) that is positive when you are safe and zero at the
boundary of the safe set. A CBF constrains the control input so that

  h_dot(x, u) >= -alpha * h(x)

In words: you may approach the boundary, but the closer you get the slower you
are allowed to approach it. As h -> 0 the permitted rate -> 0, so you never
cross. Here h is the RSS margin and u is acceleration; alpha = 1.5.


================================================================================
PART 6 - EVERY BUG WE HIT
================================================================================
This is the most valuable section in this file. In an interview, describing a
bug you found by MEASURING - not by guessing - is worth more than any feature.

Each entry: SYMPTOM -> HOW WE FOUND IT -> ROOT CAUSE -> FIX -> LESSON

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 1 - TRAFFIC SPAWNED INSIDE THE EGO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Runs occasionally started with a collision already in progress.
CAUSE    Procedural traffic was placed by random sampling with no check against
         already-placed bodies.
FIX      Rejection sampling: keep a list of claimed footprints, resample (up to
         16 attempts) until the new body is clear, and skip if the road is
         genuinely full rather than forcing it. Also reserve the ego's
         footprint PLUS the headway it needs - a scenario that starts the ego
         1 m behind a stationary bus measures nothing.
LESSON   The initial conditions are part of the experiment. A scenario that
         starts broken tells you nothing about the planner.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 2 - TIME-TO-COLLISION SATURATED AT ZERO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  minTTC was 0.00 in almost every run, so the metric was useless.
CAUSE    Collision checks used a single circumscribing disc per vehicle. For a
         12 m bus that disc has a 6 m radius, so a two-wheeler 3 m to the side
         "overlapped" it constantly.
FIX      Multi-disc body decomposition: represent each vehicle as several
         overlapping discs along its length.
LESSON   A metric that is always at its extreme value is not a metric. Check
         that your instrumentation actually varies before you trust it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 3 - THE HANDCART CRABBED SIDEWAYS AT 53 DEGREES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  A pushcart moved diagonally across the road, body pointing one way and
         travelling another. Looked absurd on screen.
CAUSE    Pushcarts were in the HOLONOMIC class list (things that can move in
         any direction without turning first, like pedestrians), and there was
         no limit on how fast heading could change.
FIX      Two changes: a heading slew-rate limit for all agents, and pushcart
         removed from HOLONOMIC_CLASSES.
LESSON   Physical plausibility is a spec, not a polish item. One visibly silly
         agent makes a viewer distrust everything else on the screen.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 4 - A 10-DEGREE BUS BECAME A 360-DEGREE OCCLUDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Sometimes the car went completely blind for a few frames.
CAUSE    Occlusion is computed as an angular interval [start, end] as seen from
         the sensor. When that interval crossed the +/-pi wraparound, the
         arithmetic produced an interval spanning the whole circle.
FIX      Unwrap the bearings before comparing.
LESSON   Every angle in a robotics codebase is a wraparound bug waiting to
         happen. We have a wrap_to_pi() helper and it is used everywhere.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 5 - A BUS TRACK SWALLOWED TWO TWO-WHEELERS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Two riders beside a bus vanished from the track list.
CAUSE    The track-merge distance was scaled by the LARGER of the two vehicles.
         Detections on one long bus do genuinely spread along its body, so the
         merge radius has to be generous - but scaled by the bus, that radius
         reached the riders next to it.
FIX      Scale by the SMALLER vehicle, and require class agreement plus a
         minimum number of hits before merging.
LESSON   In mixed traffic, "reasonable for the biggest object" is
         "catastrophic for the smallest". Always ask what a rule does at both
         extremes of your class distribution.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 6 - TWO CERTAIN CLASSES TIED AT 50/50
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  A confidently-classified object showed 0.5 confidence.
CAUSE    Class log-odds were CLAMPED before the softmax. Two scores both pinned
         at the clamp exponentiated to the same value.
FIX      Log-sum-exp softmax (subtract the max first), and bound the log-odds
         rather than clamping the exponent.
LESSON   Numerical hygiene changes behaviour, not just precision. Know the
         log-sum-exp trick; it comes up constantly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 7 - A PARKED CAR WAS PREDICTED TO CUT IN 1.8 m
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Stationary vehicles generated large cut-in hypotheses, painting risk
         across the road for no reason.
CAUSE    Lateral displacement in a manoeuvre hypothesis was a function of TIME
         only, not of how far the object would actually travel.
FIX      Bound lateral displacement by forward travel using a slip ratio
         (~tan 25 deg = 0.45 m sideways per metre forward), and treat anything
         under 0.8 m/s as stationary.
LESSON   Predictions must obey the same kinematics as the things they predict.
         Otherwise you are inventing hazards.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 8 - FULL STEERING LOCK WHILE STOPPED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The wheels snapped to full lock the moment the car stopped.
CAUSE    Pure-pursuit look-ahead distance was computed as speed x time. At
         v = 0 the look-ahead point is the car itself, so atan2(0, 0) - an
         undefined angle.
FIX      Look-ahead by DISTANCE with a floor, and fall back to the reference
         path's own heading when there is no motion.
LESSON   Every "multiply by speed" is a divide-by-zero at standstill in
         disguise. Standstill is a boundary condition worth testing explicitly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 9 - HEADING FROM atan2(d_dot, s_dot) BLEW UP AT STANDSTILL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Candidate trajectories were rejected as un-driveable exactly when the
         car was trying to pull away.
CAUSE    Heading was computed in closed form from Frenet velocities. As s_dot
         goes to zero the computed heading swings to +/-90 degrees off the
         reference, apparent curvature explodes, and everything fails
         feasibility.
FIX      Take heading from the PATH GEOMETRY (finite differences of the lifted
         world-frame points) and fall back to the reference heading when
         displacement is below 1e-3.
LESSON   Same lesson as bug 8, different formula. When you see a ratio, ask
         what happens when the denominator is zero.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 10 - THE SAFE-STOP FALLBACK COMPETED ON COST AND WON
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The car stopped far more often than it needed to.
CAUSE    The guaranteed-feasible "decelerate to stop" candidate was scored like
         any other. It has zero risk and zero jerk, so on a crowded road it was
         frequently the cheapest option.
FIX      Make it a LAST RESORT only - used when no other candidate is feasible,
         never entered into the cost comparison. Also normalised the speed cost
         so that "not moving" is properly penalised.
LESSON   A fallback that competes is not a fallback, it is a default. Keep
         emergency behaviour out of the optimiser.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 11 - SELF-LOCKING SPEED FLOORS (gridlock)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The car reached a state it could never leave.
CAUSE    Several guards were phrased as "if speed is below X, do Y", where Y
         kept speed below X. A closed loop with no exit.
FIX      Gate those guards on ego_speed > 1.0 so they cannot trigger while
         already stopped, and add follow_min_gap = 12.0 m so a leader matters
         by distance and not only by time gap (a time gap at v = 0 is
         meaningless).
LESSON   Look for cycles in your state machine's guards. "Condition implies
         action implies condition" is a deadlock.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 12 - THE BIG ONE: THE PLANNER COULD NOT PULL AWAY FROM A STOPPED BUS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the bug to tell in an interview. Learn it properly.

SYMPTOM  On the bus-stop and village-road scenarios the car would stop behind
         an obstruction and sit there for the rest of the run - 60 seconds of
         doing nothing. Behaviour said OVERTAKE. It never overtook.

HOW WE FOUND IT
         Not by tuning. The lattice already recorded, for every rejected
         candidate, WHY it was rejected. We wrote a ten-line diagnostic that
         printed a histogram of rejection reasons every two seconds:

           t=16.1s v=0.00 n=107  curvature=74  longitudinal accel=32  OK=1

         One usable trajectory out of 107. A "stuck" car is exactly what a
         planner with one option looks like from the inside.

ROOT CAUSE (two independent causes, both self-inflicted)

  (a) UNREACHABLE TERMINAL SPEEDS - 81 of 131 rejected
      We sampled terminal speeds without asking whether the vehicle could
      reach them. For the jerk-minimal quartic, peak acceleration is
      1.5*delta_v/T, so from a standstill over 1.6 s the reachable delta_v is
      only (2/3)*2.0*1.6 = 2.13 m/s. Every sample above that was built,
      scored, and thrown away by the feasibility check.

  (b) LATERAL MOTION PARAMETERISED BY TIME - 49 of 131 rejected
      The lateral polynomial advanced with the CLOCK. So a car at a standstill
      was asked to translate sideways before it had moved forward at all.
      Curvature is roughly (lateral acceleration)/v^2, so at v ~ 0 the implied
      curvature is unbounded and every offset candidate fails.

FIX
  (a) reachable_speeds(v0, a0, T, cfg) - a closed-form bound; sample inside it.
  (b) Advance the lateral profile by DISTANCE TRAVELLED instead of time. The
      longitudinal profile already tells us how far along we are, so evaluate
      the lateral polynomial at tau = T * (s(t) - s0) / total_travel. When the
      car has not moved, tau stays at 0 and no lateral motion is demanded.
      This is Werling's own low-speed formulation - the paper switches from
      d(t) to d(s) below a speed threshold for exactly this reason.

RESULT
  Usable candidates at standstill: 1 -> 35-50.
  Mean route progress across the campaign: 36% -> 43%.
  Cattle crossing and bus stop went from crashing to clean.

LESSON (say this one)
  "The fix was not a threshold. Two of our own sampling assumptions were
   generating candidates that were impossible by construction. We found it by
   counting rejection reasons instead of tuning weights - and the moment we
   had the histogram, the cause was obvious."

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 13 - RISK KERNELS SPINNING AROUND STATIONARY VEHICLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The red collision zones drawn around parked vehicles rotated wildly,
         frame to frame. The car refused to overtake a bus that was sitting
         still, apparently believing it would hit it.
HOW FOUND
         Watching the live viewer, then instrumenting: we logged the predicted
         heading for one stationary bus and compared it to its true heading of
         4 degrees. Measured: -94 deg, then -180 deg, then +58 deg.
ROOT CAUSE
         Predicted heading came from np.gradient of the predicted path. For a
         near-stationary object that path barely moves, so the gradient is
         dominated by numerical noise. The "direction" was whichever way the
         noise happened to point.
FIX      Three parts:
           * trust measured heading only above HEADING_TRUST_SPEED = 1.0 m/s
           * hold the last good heading estimate below that
           * fall back to the road heading for anything that has never moved
RESULT   Bus-stop progress 41% -> 56%; two collisions disappeared.
LESSON   Differentiating a noisy signal amplifies the noise. If you must
         differentiate, gate it on the signal being large enough to differentiate.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 14 - TWO-WHEELERS FROZE BESIDE SLOWER VEHICLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Riders alongside a car simply stopped and never moved again.
CAUSE    In NLB-IDM, when two bodies overlap LONGITUDINALLY but are laterally
         clear, the geometric gap goes negative, the IDM interaction term
         saturates, and the agent brakes at maximum for ever - even while the
         other vehicle drives away.
FIX      Skip the following term when the gap is negative AND the two are
         laterally clear.
LESSON   Car-following models assume the leader is AHEAD. In non-lane traffic
         that assumption breaks constantly.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 15 - ...AND THE FIX FOR BUG 14 CAUSED A NEW ONE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  Traffic drove into the side of our stationary vehicle at 4 m/s. Three
         of five contacts in a campaign had the ego stopped and being struck.
CAUSE    The bug-14 fix skipped the following term on ANY positive lateral gap,
         even a millimetre. A vehicle closing on a stopped one would cross the
         point where the longitudinal gap goes negative, stop braking entirely,
         and scrape down its flank.
FIX      "Laterally clear" now means clear BY A MARGIN: SIDE_CLEARANCE = 0.25 m.
         Wide enough to keep the bug-14 fix (a two-wheeler passing a bus with
         0.4 m is still fine), narrow enough to keep braking for a real scrape.
LESSON   A fix that turns a condition off entirely is usually too blunt. Ask
         "what is the smallest version of this exemption that solves the case
         I actually saw?"

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 16 - THE WRONG-WAY RIDER TURNED AROUND AND DROVE CORRECTLY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The wrong-way scenario stopped being a wrong-way scenario 10 s in.
CAUSE    Three separate faults:
           1. The policy used mirrored.d_max, which after mirroring names the
              OPPOSITE verge - so the rider hugged the wrong edge and drifted
              across.
           2. Nothing structurally prevented the heading from rotating all the
              way round.
           3. Once fixed, the rider froze head-on against oncoming traffic.
FIX      Use mirrored.d_min; add a HEADING_CONE of 70 degrees that a wrong-way
         agent may not rotate outside; give it a crawl_speed of 2.5 m/s and
         gap_seeking of 1.6 so it filters past oncoming traffic instead of
         stopping dead.
LESSON   When you mirror a coordinate frame, EVERY signed quantity flips.
         Search for all of them, not the one that broke.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 17 - CORRIDOR BOUNDARY COST MEASURED THE WRONG ROAD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The car drifted toward the verge when the reference path deviated.
CAUSE    The boundary term was evaluated in the DERIVED REFERENCE frame, but
         the corridor bounds are defined in the CORRIDOR frame. Whenever the
         derived reference bent away from the corridor centre - which is
         exactly when it matters - the two frames disagreed.
FIX      Project trajectory points back into corridor coordinates
         (corridor.to_frenet_batch) before evaluating the boundary term.
LESSON   In a system with several Frenet frames, always know WHICH frame a
         quantity is expressed in. Consider naming variables after their frame.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 18 - SPAWN CRASH: "ValueError: high - low < 0"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The benchmark campaign died on run 25 of 60.
CAUSE    Spawn code checked `if (hi - lo) < width: skip`, then sampled from
         `uniform(lo + width/2, hi - width/2)`. When hi-lo equals width to
         within floating-point rounding, the check passes but the two computed
         bounds can land in the wrong order.
FIX      Compute the inset band FIRST, then compare it: `if hi_in < lo_in:
         skip`. The test now uses exactly the values the sampler will use.
LESSON   Never validate with one expression and act with a different one that
         is only algebraically equal. Floating point is not algebra.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 19 - THE LIVE CONSOLE NEVER SHOWED REPLAN LATENCY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SYMPTOM  The REPLAN tile showed "-- ms" for the whole demo.
CAUSE    The simulator measured latency as a local variable and passed it only
         to the metrics accumulator. The websocket frame builder never saw it.
FIX      Store it on the simulator (self.last_replan_ms) and include it in the
         payload.
LESSON   The problem statement explicitly asks for replanning latency. A demo
         that cannot show the number it claims is a demo a judge is right to
         distrust. Check that your UI actually displays what you brag about.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
BUG 20 - A FAILED EXPERIMENT WE KEPT (this is a good story too)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
We hypothesised that zeroing the initial acceleration and lateral velocity
(s_ddot0, d_ddot0, d_dot0) at standstill would widen the candidate fan.

It made things WORSE: bus stop 41% -> 28%, village road 40% -> 21%, and four
new collisions. We reverted it and recorded the negative result in a commit and
a code comment.

LESSON   Keep negative results. "We tried X, it made these three numbers worse,
         here is the commit" is a stronger answer than never having tried.

================================================================================
PART 7 - PERFORMANCE ENGINEERING
================================================================================
The problem statement asks for replanning latency, so this is a scoring line,
not vanity.

WHERE THE TIME GOES (per 50 ms tick)
  risk field construction + evaluation   largest single cost
  lattice generation and scoring         second
  perception + fusion + prediction       small
  corridor DP                            small

THE THREE OPTIMISATIONS THAT MATTERED

1. VECTORISE THE RISK FIELD                          116 ms -> 18 ms
   Originally each kernel was evaluated in a Python loop over candidate points.
   Now all K kernels x N points are one broadcast numpy expression:
     delta = points[:, None, :] - mu[None, :, :]     # (N, K, 2)
   The rotation into each kernel's frame, the flat-top excess, and the Gaussian
   decay are all array operations. No Python loop touches a point.

2. BATCH THE LATTICE                                  71 ms -> 7.5 ms
   Same idea: build ALL candidate polynomials as coefficient arrays, then
   evaluate them together with one polynomial evaluation over an (M, T) grid,
   instead of looping candidate by candidate.

3. KD-TREE FOR FRENET PROJECTION                      ~34x on batch conversion
   Nearest-point-on-polyline was a loop over segments. scipy.spatial.cKDTree
   finds the nearest sample point in O(log n), then we refine locally against
   the two adjacent segments.

MEASURED RESULT
  median 95th-percentile replan time    46 ms
  8 of 10 scenarios inside the 50 ms budget that 20 Hz allows
  worst case                            72 ms (the dense market scene)

BE HONEST ABOUT THE CAVEAT: the campaign runs four simulations in parallel on
four cores, so those numbers are pessimistic by roughly a quarter compared to a
single run on an idle machine. Say that before someone asks.

THE GENERAL LESSON
  "Python is slow" is not the problem. Python LOOPS are the problem. Every
  optimisation here was the same move: turn a loop over objects into one array
  operation over all objects. We did not rewrite anything in C.

THE TESTS WORTH DESCRIBING (these are behavioural, not unit tests)

      Runs every scenario and asserts no two footprints overlap at t=0. This is

      Runs the whole loop twice with the same seed and asserts identical

      rather than anecdotal.

  test_chaos_increases_hazard_population
  test_lane_markings_degrade_with_chaos
      Assert the difficulty knob actually does something.

THE BENCHMARK HARNESS               scripts/benchmark.py
  10 scenarios x 3 seeds x 2 controllers = 60 runs, in a process pool.
  Both controllers see IDENTICAL seeds and IDENTICAL sensor noise.
  Writes artifacts/benchmark.json - one row per run plus a summary.
  Each failed run is recorded as a failed run rather than killing the campaign
  (we learned that when bug 18 killed run 25 of 60).

WHY THE BASELINE MATTERS
  BaselineLaneFollower is a conventional lane-following planner. It is a FAIR
  comparison, not a straw man: same sensors, same noise, same seeds. It mostly
  fails by STOPPING rather than by crashing - which is exactly why the
  collision counts are close while the progress numbers are not.

REPRODUCIBILITY AS A DESIGN RULE
  Both presentation decks read benchmark.json at build time and REFUSE TO BUILD
  without it. A number on a slide cannot drift away from what the code does,
  because no number on a slide was ever typed by hand.

FAULT ATTRIBUTION IN COLLISIONS
  Every contact records our own speed and the BEARING of the other body
  relative to our nose. That separates "we drove into it" from "we were
  stationary and were struck", which are different failures needing different
  fixes. A raw collision count cannot tell you which you have.

PART 10 - BUILD, PACKAGING AND TOOLING DECISIONS
================================================================================

WHY uv (and what to say if asked)
  uv is a Rust-based Python package and project manager. We use it because the
  single biggest failure mode of a student demo is "it doesn't run on the
  judge's laptop".

    git clone ...
    cd automated-driving
    uv sync          # creates .venv, resolves and installs everything
    uv run sarathi serve

  uv fetches the correct Python interpreter itself (3.11, pinned in
  .python-version), so the user does not need Python installed at all.
  uv.lock is committed, so the resolution is identical on every machine.

PACKAGING DETAILS WORTH KNOWING
  * Build backend: hatchling. Declared in [build-system].
  * [project.scripts] sarathi = "sarathi.cli:main" creates the `sarathi`
    command on install - that is why `uv run sarathi serve` works.
  * force-include maps scenarios/ into the wheel as sarathi/_scenarios, so the
    scenario library ships INSIDE the package. That means
    `uvx --from git+... sarathi serve` works with no checkout on disk.
  * paths.py resolves scenarios in priority order: explicit --scenarios-dir,
    then ./scenarios in the working directory, then the bundled copy.
  * Optional dev extra (pytest, playwright, pillow, python-pptx) so a plain
    install stays small: `uv run --extra dev pytest`.

THE LIVE DEMO ARCHITECTURE            sarathi/serve.py + assets/mission_control.html
  * One process. A background thread serves the static HTML on port P; the
    simulation runs and pushes frames over a websocket on port P+1.
  * The viewer is ONE HTML file with no build step and no dependencies. Nothing
    to install, nothing to compile, no node_modules on the demo path.
  * The SAME renderer serves two data sources: an embedded recorded run
    (replay mode) or the live socket. One code path, two modes.
  * LiveSession exposes load / step / place / remove / grab / drag / drop /
    tune / reset_tuning. Dragging is grab -> move_held -> release, with the
    held agent excluded from the physics update while held.
  * Sessions never halt (the `live` flag), so a demo can be left running.

WHY A LIVE DEMO AT ALL - the argument to make
  "A recorded video invites the question 'how do we know it isn't a replay?'.
   Someone who places the obstacle themselves has already answered it. The code
   answering that click is the code in the submission."

THE THRESHOLD PANEL
  31 tunables in five groups (driving, caution, planner, safety, ablations),
  all adjustable while the vehicle drives, defined once in tuning.py with
  min/max/step/description and rendered generically by the viewer. The
  ablation switches turn off the risk field, the prediction, or RSS so the
  contribution of each is VISIBLE rather than asserted.

FIGURE CAPTURE                        scripts/capture.py
  Starts the real server, drives real Chromium via Playwright, and POLLS THE
  TELEMETRY until it satisfies a condition you give on the command line
  (behaviour in a set, path clearance above a threshold, speed above a
  threshold, enough elapsed time) before it takes the screenshot. So a figure
  in the deck is a moment we CHOSE, not a moment we happened to catch.

GIT HYGIENE (a real lesson from this project)
  main once contained 38 .pyc files and no source, because a push went wrong.
  Now: __pycache__/ and *.pyc and node_modules/ are ignored at the repo root,
  and artifacts/ is ignored EXCEPT for benchmark.json and the eight figures the
  decks embed - the evidence has to be in the repo or a reviewer cannot check
  it.

PART 11 - WHAT IS STILL WRONG (say these before you are asked)
================================================================================

1. STILL TOO CAUTIOUS IN DENSE TRAFFIC
   Mean route progress is 43%. On the hardest scenarios the car spends a lot of
   the run stopped. The standstill sampling bug (bug 12) was the biggest cause
   and is fixed; what remains is genuine conservatism in the cost weights and
   the hard-constraint probability threshold.

2. FIVE OF THIRTY RUNS END IN CONTACT
   Concentrated in the unsignalled junction and the night wrong-way scenario.
   Three of the five had the ego stationary and were struck. The other two are
   ours.

3. THE DENSE MARKET EXCEEDS THE LATENCY BUDGET
   72 ms against a 50 ms budget, because that scene has the most agents and the
   risk field cost scales with agent count. The obvious fix is to cap the
   number of kernels by distance and relevance; we have not done it.

4. PREDICTION PRIORS ARE HAND-BUILT
   Not fitted to data. Defensible, but the first thing a research-minded
   interviewer will push on. The answer: fit them per class from IDD tracks.

5. NO MATLAB / SIMULINK YET
   The behaviour machine is deliberately Stateflow-shaped and the scenario
   files are designed to drive both runtimes, but the port is NOT WRITTEN. Do
   not claim it. The matlab/ directory is empty and the resume does not list
   MATLAB for exactly this reason.

6. NO CI
   There is no GitHub Actions workflow. 88 tests exist and pass locally. Do not
   say "tests run in CI" - we had that sentence in a slide and removed it.

--- ABOUT THE IDEA ---

Q: Explain your project in one minute.
A: PART 1. Learn it verbatim.

Q: Isn't this just the Frenet planner from the Automated Driving Toolbox
   example?
A: That example is one of our eight stages. It assumes a reference line and
   hands you a lattice. Our contribution is what feeds it: a corridor solved
   from free space instead of read from paint, and a risk field that is
   class-conditioned, harm-weighted and indexed by time. The example has
   neither.

Q: Isn't your risk field just a blurred occupancy grid?
A: A grid is binary, class-blind and single-time. Ours is continuous,
   class-conditioned (a cow's kernel is not a barricade's), harm-weighted (a
   pedestrian costs more than sheet metal), and indexed by time so a trajectory
   is scored against where things WILL be. Blur gives you none of those four.

Q: Why not end-to-end learning?
A: Two reasons. There is no Indian trajectory dataset large enough to train a
   safe policy. And when it fails you cannot explain why, which makes it
   unusable for anything a regulator has to sign off. Our failure modes are
   inspectable: I can show you the histogram of why each candidate trajectory
   was rejected.

Q: What is actually novel here?
A: Three things. Deriving the planning reference from free space rather than
   markings, so there is no "markings missing" branch anywhere in the code. A
   risk field that is explicitly harm-weighted. And a traffic model that is
   non-lane-based, so the thing we test against behaves like Indian traffic
   rather than like a queue.

--- ABOUT THE ENGINEERING ---

Q: How fast does it run, and how do you know?
A: 20 Hz closed loop. Median 95th-percentile replan across ten scenarios is
   46 ms, 8 of 10 inside the 50 ms budget, worst case 72 ms in the densest
   scene. Measured by scripts/benchmark.py, which also notes that the campaign
   runs four simulations in parallel, so those numbers are pessimistic.

Q: What was the hardest bug?
A: Bug 12. Tell the whole story: symptom, the rejection histogram, the two
   independent causes, the closed-form fix, and the 36% -> 43% result.

Q: How did you test something like this?
A: 88 tests, but the ones that matter are behavioural: every scenario spawns
   cleanly, every scenario is deterministic under a fixed seed, and the
   difficulty knob provably changes the scene. Plus a Monte-Carlo campaign
   against a baseline on identical seeds - which is what turns "it looked good"
   into a number.

Q: What would you optimise next?
A: Cap the risk-field kernel count by distance and relevance, which is the
   72 ms market case. Then revisit the hard-constraint probability threshold,
   which is the main remaining source of conservatism.

Q: Why Python? Isn't it too slow for real-time?
A: It is fast enough here because nothing hot is a Python loop - the risk field
   and the lattice are both single vectorised numpy expressions over all
   objects. That took the field from 116 ms to 18 ms and the lattice from 71 ms
   to 7.5 ms without leaving Python. For a vehicle you would port the hot path,
   and the structure is already the right one to port because it is array
   maths, not object graphs.

Q: How would this run on a real car?
A: 20 Hz on a laptop CPU with no GPU, so compute is not the gap. The gap is
   sensors and integration: real detections instead of modelled ones, a real
   localisation source for the corridor, and a vehicle interface. The planner
   interface would not change - it already consumes tracks, not ground truth.

--- ABOUT THE NUMBERS ---

Q: Is it safer than the baseline?
A: Not yet by raw contact count - 25 of 30 runs clean against 27 of 30. It is
   substantially better at making progress: 43% against 36%, ahead on 8 of 10
   scenarios. And three of our five contacts happened with the vehicle
   stationary and struck by someone else, which we can show because every
   contact records our speed and the impact bearing.

Q: So it doesn't really work yet?
A: It drives every scenario and is collision-free in most runs. Where it is
   weak it is weak by being SLOW rather than by being unsafe, which is the
   failure mode we chose. I can show you the exact cause on screen right now,
   and the remaining fix is a sampling and weighting change, not a rewrite.

Q: Why does it stop so much?
A: It was much worse, and I can tell you exactly why it was: 81 of 131
   candidates rejected for unreachable acceleration and 49 for curvature, from
   a standstill. That is fixed. What remains is the cost weighting and the
   probability threshold above which a predicted manoeuvre becomes a hard
   constraint - set too low, the union of everything anyone MIGHT do covers the
   whole road.

--- ABOUT PROCESS ---

Q: Did you use AI to write this?
A: Be straightforward. Something like: "Yes, heavily - I used Claude Code as a
   pair programmer. I directed the architecture, ran the thing, found bugs by
   watching it, and made the calls about what to fix and what to measure. The
   rotating-kernel bug and the wrong-way U-turn are both ones I spotted by
   running it locally and describing what I saw." Then IMMEDIATELY demonstrate
   understanding by explaining a mechanism in depth - that is what actually
   settles the question. Nobody minds the tool; they mind if you cannot explain
   the output.

Q: What would you do differently?
A: Instrument earlier. The rejection-reason histogram took ten lines and found
   in five minutes a bug we had been tuning around for days. I would build the
   measurement harness before the planner next time, not after.

Q: What did you learn?
A: Three things. Measure before you tune - every threshold I changed by
   intuition made things worse, and the one diagnostic I wrote found the real
   cause immediately. Keep the fallback out of the optimiser. And in mixed
   traffic, every rule you write has to be checked at both ends of the size
   distribution, because what is reasonable for a bus is catastrophic for a
   two-wheeler.

PART 13 - TRAPS THAT WILL CATCH YOU OUT
================================================================================

* Do NOT say "it always works". Five of thirty runs end in contact.
* Do NOT say "that's just a simulation artefact".
* Do NOT say "we'll fix that later" without saying when and how.
* Do NOT claim MATLAB/Simulink work. It is not written.
* Do NOT claim CI. There is no workflow.
* Do NOT quote a number that is not in PART 9. If asked for one you do not
  have, say "we have not measured that; here is how we would" - that answer
  costs nothing.
* Do NOT describe the baseline as a straw man. It sees identical sensors,
  noise and seeds, and it beats us on two scenarios.
* If someone points at a prediction cone spilling off the road: the mean path
  is propagated along the corridor, but the cone also draws lateral
  UNCERTAINTY, and uncertainty is not confined to the road. A pedestrian
  stepping off the verge is exactly the case we must not model away.
* If the demo breaks: reset the vehicle, keep talking. If the browser dies,
  reload - the session survives. If the server dies, second terminal, same
  command. If everything dies, use the recorded replay page and SAY that is
  what you are doing.

PART 14 - GLOSSARY
================================================================================

Frenet frame        Coordinates along a reference line: s = distance along,
                    d = offset sideways.
Jerk                Rate of change of acceleration. What passengers feel as a
                    lurch.
Jerk-minimal        The polynomial connecting two states with least integrated
                    squared jerk. Quintic when both endpoints are fully
                    specified; quartic when the terminal position is free.
Lattice             The fan of candidate trajectories generated and scored each
                    tick.
Corridor            The ribbon of drivable free space our dynamic program
                    finds. Our replacement for the lane.
Risk field          Continuous cost over space AND time; class-conditioned and
                    harm-weighted.
Anisotropic kernel  A risk blob that is not a circle - longer along the
                    direction of travel than across it.
Flat-top kernel     A kernel that is uniformly maximal inside the body
                    (configuration-space core) and decays outside it.
Configuration space The obstacle grown by our own footprint, so the vehicle can
                    be planned for as a single point.
RSS                 Responsibility-Sensitive Safety. A formal minimum-gap
                    condition; we invert it to get a safe speed.
CBF                 Control barrier function. A filter guaranteeing you stay
                    inside a safe set by slowing your approach to its boundary.
TTC                 Time to collision on current relative motion.
IDM                 Intelligent Driver Model - standard car-following model.
NLB-IDM             Non-lane-based IDM: interaction weighted by lateral overlap
                    instead of lane membership.
Pseudo-lane         The lateral influence width a vehicle claims, which grows
                    with its speed.
Log-odds            log(p/(1-p)). Lets evidence ADD instead of multiply.
Ablation            Switching a component off to show what it was contributing.
Chaos               Our scenario difficulty dial, 0..1: wrong-way riders,
                    aggression, cattle, barricades, marking visibility.
Coasting            Continuing to predict a track after detections stop, before
                    deleting it.
Determinism         Same scenario + same seed = identical run. What makes the
                    campaign meaningful.


================================================================================
PART 15 - TEN THINGS TO REMEMBER
================================================================================

1.  We did not add Indian conditions to a Western planner. We removed the
    assumption the Western planner is built on.
2.  The lane centre line became a corridor solved by dynamic programming over
    free space, re-solved 20 times a second.
3.  The occupancy grid became a continuous, class-conditioned, harm-weighted,
    time-indexed risk field.
4.  Prediction is multi-modal because the average of two futures metres apart
    is confidently wrong.
5.  The safety supervisor is MONOTONE - it can only slow us down - which is why
    no upstream tuning mistake can make the vehicle more aggressive.
6.  The planner never sees ground truth. It sees noisy, occluded, sometimes
    misclassified tracks.
7.  The biggest bug was found by counting why candidates were rejected, not by
    tuning: 1 usable trajectory out of 131 at a standstill.
8.  Every number comes out of one committed script, on identical seeds for both
    controllers.
9.  43% route progress against the baseline's 36%, ahead on 8 of 10 scenarios -
    and 25/30 clean against its 27/30, which we volunteer rather than hide.
10. The demo is live. Someone else places the obstacle. That is the whole
    argument.
